# weise2016chaparral

## Citation
Weise, D. R., Koo, E., Zhou, X., Mahalingam, S., Morandini, F., & Balbi, J.-H. (2016). Fire spread in chaparral - a comparison of laboratory data and model predictions in burning live fuels. *International Journal of Wildland Fire*, 25(9), 980-994. https://doi.org/10.1071/wf15177

## Publication status
PEER_REVIEWED. Crossref-verified. Evidence level **E2** — abstract only.

## Problem
Do operational and physical fire spread models predict spread in *live* fuels, where the Rothermel model was never
calibrated?

## Method
240 laboratory fires in high-density live chaparral fuel beds. Logistic regression for spread success; linear
regression for rate of spread. Compared predictions from the Rothermel equation, three proposed modifications
(Cohen, Wilson, Catchpole), and two physically based models.

## Data
240 laboratory fires; live chaparral.

## Outputs
- **"The Rothermel model failed to predict fire spread in nearly all of the fires that spread using default values."**
- Increasing the moisture of extinction improved performance only marginally.
- The models successfully predicted fire spread **49% to 69% of the time**.
- **Only the physical model predictions fell within a factor of two of actual rates.**
- Mean bias of most models was close to zero (i.e. the failure is variance and discrimination, not offset).
- Wind was the most important variable for spread success; air temperature, live fuel moisture, slope angle and
  bulk density were significantly related to spread rate.

## Key equations
Rothermel plus the Cohen/Wilson/Catchpole modifications; not reproduced.

## Assumptions
Laboratory fuel beds represent live chaparral behaviour.

## Validation
This is a model-evaluation study.

## Limitations
Laboratory scale; chaparral, not Korean pine. But the *mechanism* of failure — a model calibrated on dead,
uniform, laboratory fuels applied to live, structured fuel — transfers directly to the Korean *Pinus densiflora*
case, which is the point for us.

## WildfireGuardian overlap
Provides the sharpest single statement of spread-model failure (not merely error) and supports the argument that
WildfireGuardian must treat forecast error as potentially catastrophic, not merely noisy.
